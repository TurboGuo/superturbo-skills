# Overclaiming: the patterns reviewers catch

## 1. The verb upgrade between sections
Results say "is associated with", the discussion says "increases", the abstract
says "drives". Check all three against each other before submission. This is the
most common overclaim and the easiest to catch yourself.

## 2. The implication leap
Cross-sectional survey → "organizations should invest in X". The recommendation
is a causal claim. Either the design supports it or the recommendation is
reframed as "settings where X is higher also show Y, which suggests X is worth
investigating as a lever".

## 3. HARKing
A hypothesis that predicts exactly what was found, including the unexpected
interaction, with no preregistration. Reviewers detect it by the suspicious
specificity of hypotheses that no prior theory would have generated.
Fix: label post-hoc analyses as exploratory. Exploratory findings are
publishable; disguised ones are a retraction risk.

## 4. p-value theatre
`p < .001` with no effect size, no confidence interval, and a sample of 4,000
where everything is significant. Report the estimate, the interval, and a
substantive benchmark.

## 5. "Trending toward significance"
p = .07 is not a trend. Either the interval is informative about effects the
field cares about, or the study is underpowered. Say which.

## 6. Generalizing a LATE
An instrument identifies the effect among compliers. The abstract says "the
effect of X on Y". Name the estimand in the abstract.

## 7. The mechanism assertion
A mediation coefficient from cross-sectional data is presented as evidence of a
mechanism. Cross-sectional mediation is nearly always uninterpretable — the
mediator and outcome are measured at the same moment, so the temporal order the
mediation model assumes is not in the data.

## 8. The buried limitation
The real threat is listed fourth, after "the sample was from one country" and
"self-report measures were used". Reviewers read limitations for what is missing
from them.

## 9. Prevalence claims from qualitative data
"Most participants felt..." from 18 interviews. Qualitative work generalizes to
theory. Use `many participants described` at most, and prefer naming the pattern
rather than counting it.

## 10. The unfalsifiable contribution
A framework that accommodates every possible outcome. Ask what result would have
disconfirmed it. If none, it is a vocabulary, not a theory.

## 11. Citing your own hedge as evidence
"As noted, this relationship may be causal (Author, 2021)" where Author 2021 is
the same authors making the same hedge. Circular support is visible in the
reference list.

## 12. The null presented as support
A non-significant interaction described as "the effect did not differ by group,
supporting generalizability". A null interaction in an underpowered test
supports nothing. Report the interval and the power.
